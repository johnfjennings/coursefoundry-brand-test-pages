Junior Narrative Designer Case Study: Elden Ring

Author

Mr. John Jennings

Introduction

This lesson examines how narrative design works in practice, using Elden Ring as a critical case study before applying those lessons to an original creative proposal. You will explore how the game connects world design, gameplay, and story, then see how the same principles guide the design of a new DLC expansion, “The Shattered Citadel.” The lesson closes with a reflection on the “show, don’t tell” philosophy and how it shaped this creative work.

Critical Case Analysis: Ludonarrative Harmony in Elden Ring

Elden Ring tells its story mostly through the world and gameplay rather than through direct exposition. Players explore, fight bosses, and choose their own path, and this way of playing matches the story itself: the Lands Between is a dangerous and mysterious place, so exploring and learning fits the narrative.

The game presents a broken and ruined world. Kingdoms have fallen, powerful demigods have become corrupted, and the natural order has collapsed. The player takes on the role of a Tarnished, once exiled but now called back to try to restore the Elden Ring.

The gameplay reflects this story directly. The world is dangerous and unforgiving — enemies are strong, bosses are difficult, and death happens often. This difficulty is not random; it reinforces the idea that the world is damaged and unstable. The player must learn from mistakes, grow stronger, and slowly come to understand the world. This alignment between what the player does and what the story communicates is known as ludonarrative harmony.

For example, when a player fights a powerful demigod like Godrick or Radahn, the difficulty of the fight demonstrates how powerful these characters are within the story. Their strength in gameplay reflects their importance in the narrative, and the challenge makes the world feel real and serious.

However, moments of ludonarrative dissonance can also occur. Sometimes certain enemies or bosses feel difficult without a clear story explanation — a random strong enemy in a small area might feel more like a gameplay obstacle than part of the narrative world. When this happens, the player may feel frustrated rather than immersed, and the emotional connection to the story weakens as the focus shifts from meaning to mechanics.

Overall, Elden Ring mostly succeeds in creating harmony between gameplay and story. The open world, the danger, and the sense of mystery all support the narrative themes of decay, ambition, and struggle.

Storytelling Techniques

Elden Ring uses both explicit and environmental storytelling, but it relies far more heavily on environmental methods.

Explicit storytelling is limited. There are some cutscenes, especially during boss introductions or major story moments, and NPCs speak to the player and provide hints about quests. However, the dialogue is often short and unclear, with characters speaking in mysterious or poetic ways rather than explaining events directly. This design choice makes the player work harder to understand the story, paying attention to small details instead of relying on long cinematic scenes.

Environmental storytelling is the strongest storytelling method in the game. The world itself tells the story. For example, exploring Stormveil Castle reveals broken walls, dead soldiers, and signs of battle, showing that the castle was once powerful but has now fallen into chaos. In Caelid, the sky, the diseased land, and strange creatures show the impact of Scarlet Rot without needing long explanations. Even item descriptions contribute to this method, teaching players about past events, characters, and wars through small details. This approach follows the principle of “show, don’t tell”: instead of explaining everything, the game allows players to experience the consequences of past events through the environment.

Player choice versus story is also carefully balanced. Elden Ring gives players a high level of freedom — they can explore areas in different orders, choose which bosses to fight first, and build their characters around strength, magic, faith, dexterity, or hybrid combinations. This freedom gives players a sense of control and personal journey. However, the main story outcomes remain authored by the developers; the player cannot completely change the world’s history, and the endings are pre-written options. This balance between freedom and structure allows players to feel independent while still following a meaningful story.

Creative Design Proposal: The Shattered Citadel

World-Building

  • Setting: A floating citadel that only appears at night, where gravity changes and platforms float.
  • Rules: Gravity shifts in certain areas, platforms float in a set pattern, and magic behaves unpredictably.
  • History: The citadel once belonged to a sorcerer family. Torn banners, journals, and broken towers tell the story of its fall.
  • Conflict: The citadel’s unstable magic threatens the Lands Between, and the player must find a way to fix it.

The Shattered Citadel fits naturally within the world of Elden Ring because it builds on themes already present in the base game — ambition, decay, and consequence. By combining environmental storytelling, a strong character arc, and a mechanic directly tied to the area’s history, the DLC maintains internal consistency while introducing something new to the Lands Between.

Character and Motivation

Lyra, the last remaining guardian of the Shattered Citadel, once served the sorcerer dynasty that ruled it and believed strongly in their goal of controlling time. When their experiments failed and the citadel shattered, she survived but became trapped in the time loop they created, leaving her feeling responsible for what happened.

Visual design and silhouette: Lyra wears dark silver armour with deep blue details that glow faintly. Her most distinctive feature is an hourglass-shaped helmet, inside which faint golden sand slowly falls, symbolising the unstable time magic of the citadel. She carries a long curved blade infused with time energy, and her cape flickers slightly as she moves, as if not fully stable in the present moment. Her silhouette is tall and elegant but slightly fragmented, reflecting the broken state of the citadel — and the hourglass helmet makes her instantly recognisable from a distance, an important quality in character design.

Motivation: At the start of the DLC, Lyra does not trust the player, believing outsiders caused the citadel’s fall. She sees herself as its final protector and refuses to accept that the sorcerers were responsible for their own downfall. Her goal is to protect what remains of the citadel and keep its knowledge safe, though she is also weary of being trapped in an endless loop.

Character arc: As the player explores the citadel and uncovers the truth about the failed experiments, Lyra slowly realises that the sorcerers’ ambition caused the disaster, and she begins to question whether protecting the citadel is truly right. By the end of the DLC, she accepts that the citadel must either be stabilised in a new way or allowed to end. If the player chooses to break the time loop, Lyra finally becomes free from her duty. Her arc reflects a theme of acceptance — that trying to control time and preserve the past can cause more harm than good.

Mechanics and Narrative Link

New mechanic: Time-shift areas change depending on the player’s actions, with enemies and platforms moving differently in response.

Why it fits the story: This mechanic makes players feel that the citadel itself is unstable, matching the story’s themes of discovery and danger.

Reflection

“Show, don’t tell” is often more effective in games than in films or books because games are interactive. In a film, the audience watches the story; in a game, the player experiences it. This difference matters. When a game shows something through gameplay or environment instead of explaining it directly, the player feels more connected to the story.

In Elden Ring, the world tells its story through ruins, enemies, item descriptions, and boss fights rather than long explanations. Exploring a destroyed castle or land affected by Scarlet Rot lets the player understand that something terrible happened without needing a cutscene. This creates mystery, encourages curiosity, and makes the player active in discovering meaning rather than passively receiving it.

This idea shaped the design of the Shattered Citadel DLC. Rather than explaining everything directly, the design focuses on environmental storytelling — broken hourglasses, unstable gravity, and magic cracks in the sky all show that time magic went wrong, letting the player learn history by exploring rather than being told.

The Hero’s Journey also influenced Lyra’s character design. In many hero stories, a mentor or guardian figure helps guide the protagonist, often resisting change at first. Lyra’s initial distrust of the player reflects the “refusal of the call” stage, and her development mirrors the idea that growth comes from facing reality instead of avoiding it.

Overall, this project highlighted that strong narrative design is not just about writing a good story — it is about connecting gameplay, world design, and character development so that everything supports the same theme. When mechanics and story work together, the experience feels deeper and more meaningful.

Visuals

Two concept visuals were created to support the DLC: one for the environment and one for the main NPC. These visuals help communicate the story, themes, and gameplay ideas without relying on long text explanations, making it easier to see how the Shattered Citadel works and how Lyra connects to its story.

Shattered Citadel — Environment Concept: The Shattered Citadel is depicted as floating and broken, showing that time in this area is unstable. Towers and platforms are suspended in the air, creating a sense of danger and mystery. Glowing cracks and drifting golden sand represent the effects of failed time experiments by the sorcerers. The design also supports gameplay, since floating platforms can create puzzles and Time Shift challenges. The environment communicates a story of ambition, failure, and chaos without dialogue or cutscenes, helping the player understand the DLC’s theme simply by looking around.

Lyra, the Timebound Guardian — Character Concept: Lyra’s hourglass-shaped helmet clearly signals her connection to the time loop. Her glowing sword and partially broken cape reflect the unstable magic of the citadel, while the blue and silver colour palette visually links her to the environment. The design communicates that she is both a guardian and a prisoner of the broken time loop. Her distinct silhouette ensures players recognise her immediately, reinforcing her character arc and connecting her visually to the gameplay mechanics and the wider story.

Summary

Elden Ring achieves much of its emotional power through ludonarrative harmony, using difficulty, exploration, and environmental detail to reinforce its themes of decay, ambition, and struggle, while occasional moments of dissonance show how easily that connection can break. Its heavy reliance on environmental storytelling over explicit exposition, combined with a careful balance between player freedom and authored story structure, offers a model for narrative design. Applying these same principles to the Shattered Citadel DLC — through world-building, a character arc built on acceptance, and a mechanic tied directly to the setting’s history — demonstrates how “show, don’t tell” can be carried from analysis into original creative practice, ultimately reinforcing that strong narrative design depends on gameplay, world, and character all supporting a single unified theme.
